When credit cards, a car loan and a line of credit are all competing for your paycheque, the equity in your home can look like an easy fix. Often it's a real improvement. It can also quietly cost more over the long run.

## Three ways to use your equity

**Refinance.** You replace your mortgage with a larger one and use the extra to pay off the debts. In Canada you can generally refinance up to 80% of your home's value. You re-qualify under current rules, and your whole mortgage moves to today's rate, which may be higher or lower than your current one.

**HELOC.** A revolving line of credit secured by your home. At federally regulated lenders the revolving portion is capped at 65% of the home's value, and the HELOC combined with your mortgage can reach up to 80%. Minimum payments are often interest only, which is convenient and is also the risk.

**Second mortgage.** A separate loan registered behind your existing mortgage. It leaves your current mortgage and rate untouched, which can make sense when breaking it would be expensive. Second mortgages usually cost more, have shorter terms, and often come from alternative or private lenders with fees on top.

## Costs to count first

- **The penalty** for breaking your current term early. On a fixed rate this can be large, so get the exact figure from your lender.
- **Legal fees and an appraisal**, and possibly a discharge fee.
- **Lender or brokerage fees**, common on second mortgages.

## An illustrative before and after

Round, illustrative numbers, not a quote. They assume an illustrative 5% rate on the new borrowing over 25 years and, to keep it simple, that your existing mortgage payment doesn't change.

| Monthly payment | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing mortgage | $2,000 | $2,000 |
| Credit cards ($25,000) | $750 | $0 |
| Car loan ($20,000) | $450 | $0 |
| Line of credit ($15,000) | $300 | $0 |
| Added to mortgage ($60,000 plus about $5,000 in costs) | none | about $380 |
| **Total** | **$3,500** | **about $2,380** |

That's roughly $1,120 a month of breathing room, and for a household under strain it can make a real difference.

## The trap: 25 years for short-term debt

That car loan would have been gone in about four years. Now it's part of a 25-year mortgage. In this example, paying about $380 a month for 25 years on $65,000 adds up to roughly $48,000 in interest. The rate and the payment are lower, but the total interest can easily end up higher than if you'd kept making the original payments.

The fix is simple to describe and harder to do: keep paying close to what you paid before, and send the difference to the mortgage as prepayments, within your prepayment privileges. Here, your $380 payment plus about $1,120 a month in prepayments would clear the $65,000 in roughly four years, with total interest closer to $7,000. The [prepayment calculator](/calculator/prepayment) shows what different amounts would do.

## Deal with what created the debt

Consolidation fixes the interest rate, not the habit. If the spending that filled the cards hasn't changed, it's common to end up with new balances on top of a bigger mortgage. A one-time event like a job loss is different from ongoing spending above income. A written budget, and closing or lowering the limits on paid-off cards, both help.

## When it's not a good idea

- The debts could be cleared within a year or so from regular cash flow
- Your penalty is large and your term ends soon (waiting may be cheaper, as covered in [Refinancing 101](/blog/refinancing-when-it-makes-sense))
- The spending behind the debt hasn't changed
- Your income is unstable, since you're turning unsecured debt into debt secured by your home
- The debt is larger than your equity can realistically solve, in which case a non-profit credit counsellor may be a better first call
