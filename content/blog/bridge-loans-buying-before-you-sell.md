Moving up in Toronto usually means juggling two deals at once. Ideally your sale closes first and the proceeds go straight into the next purchase. In practice the dates don't always line up. When your purchase closes before your sale, a bridge loan is the usual way to cover the gap.

## What a bridge loan actually does

Most of the down payment for your next home is probably tied up in the home you live in now, and you can't get at it until that sale closes. A bridge loan lends you that equity for the days or weeks between the two closings. When your sale closes, your lawyer uses the proceeds to repay it in full.

Bridge financing is usually arranged by the lender providing the mortgage on your new home, as part of the same approval, so raise it early.

## What lenders usually require

- **A firm sale.** Most lenders want a signed, unconditional agreement of purchase and sale on your current home. A listing, or an offer that still has conditions, generally isn't enough.
- **A short gap.** Many lenders cap bridge loans at around 90 days, and some allow 120 days or a bit longer. Policies vary by lender.
- **Enough equity.** The loan is sized on what you'll actually walk away with: the sale price minus your current mortgage, the real estate commission and other selling costs.
- **A lawyer coordinating both closings.** Your lawyer repays the bridge from the sale proceeds, so they need the details from the start.

## What it costs

A bridge loan is usually priced at the lender's prime rate plus a margin, commonly with a one-time setup or administration fee on top. Because the loan is short, the dollar cost is often modest next to the size of the move, but the rate is higher than your mortgage rate. Ask for the rate and the fee in writing.

During the gap you're also carrying two homes, and the closing costs on your purchase, including land transfer tax, are due on the purchase date. The [closing costs calculator](/calculator/closing-costs) estimates them for your price.

## A simple example

Round numbers, for illustration only:

- You've sold your current home for $1,000,000, firm, closing September 30.
- Your mortgage balance is $400,000, and commission plus selling costs come to about $50,000, so you expect roughly $550,000 net.
- You've bought your next home, closing August 31, and plan to put $500,000 of the sale proceeds toward it.

A $500,000 bridge loan for 30 days covers that down payment on August 31. Interest on $500,000 for 30 days works out to about $410 for every percentage point of annual rate. If the bridge rate were 7% (a figure chosen for easy math, not a quote), that's roughly $2,900 in interest, plus the setup fee. On September 30 your sale closes and the bridge is repaid.

## If your sale isn't firm yet

This is the harder situation, and it's worth thinking through before you make an offer.

- **Align the closing dates.** Negotiate a longer closing on the purchase, or a shorter one on the sale, so the sale closes first or both close the same day.
- **Make the purchase conditional on your sale.** This protects you, though sellers in a competitive market may not accept it.
- **Use a line of credit.** A HELOC on your current home can fund the down payment in the meantime, if you qualify to carry both properties.
- **Private bridge financing.** Some private lenders will bridge without a firm sale. It costs noticeably more and needs a realistic repayment plan.

Each of these shifts risk to you. If your current home takes longer to sell, or sells for less, you could be carrying two properties with no fixed end date. Stress-test that scenario honestly before you buy first.
