A bank declining your mortgage application isn't the end of the road. It usually means your file doesn't fit that lender's rules right now. Alternative and private lenders exist for exactly those situations. They can be genuinely useful, and expensive if you go in without a plan.

## Who ends up outside the banks

- **Bruised credit.** Missed payments, collections, a consumer proposal or a recent bankruptcy.
- **Income that's hard to document.** Newly self-employed borrowers, commission earners, or business owners whose write-offs shrink their taxable income.
- **Speed.** A deadline that's too tight for a traditional approval.
- **The property itself.** Rural land, a home that needs major work, or a property type many lenders avoid.

## A, B and private lenders

| Lender type | Who they are | What to expect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A lenders | Big banks, monoline lenders and many credit unions | The lowest rates and the strictest qualifying rules |
| B (alternative) lenders | Trust companies and other alternative lenders | More flexibility on credit and income, higher rates, often a lender fee |
| Private lenders | Individuals and mortgage investment corporations (MICs) | A focus on the property and your equity, the highest cost, short terms |

A B lender is often the better next step after a bank says no, since it usually costs less than private money.

## What private mortgages commonly look like

Every private lender sets its own terms, but these features are common:

- **Short terms.** Often one year, sometimes shorter or a little longer.
- **Interest-only payments.** Your payment covers interest, not principal, so the balance doesn't go down.
- **Lower loan-to-value limits.** Many private lenders lend up to somewhere around 65% to 75% of the property's value, sometimes less.
- **Fees on top of the rate.** Expect a lender fee and, unlike most bank mortgages, a brokerage fee paid by you, both commonly a percentage of the loan. Legal and appraisal costs come on top.
- **Renewal fees.** If you need more time, renewing often means another fee.

Because the fees are paid up front, a one-year private mortgage costs more than the rate alone suggests. Compare offers in total dollars.

## Everything in writing before you commit

Mortgage brokerages in Ontario are regulated by the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA). Before you commit, you receive written disclosure of the cost of borrowing, including the fees. Read it closely, have your own lawyer review the documents, and don't sign until the numbers match what you were told.

## The exit plan matters most

A private mortgage should be a bridge to something, not a place to stay. Before signing, you should be able to answer one question plainly: how, and when, do I get out?

Common exits include:

- **Rebuilding credit** so a B or A lender will take over.
- **Building the income history** a conventional lender needs, such as another year of self-employed tax returns.
- **Selling the property** on your own timeline rather than in a rush.
- **Finishing renovations** so the property qualifies with a mainstream lender.

If your exit only works when everything goes perfectly, build in some margin, and know what a renewal would cost if it takes longer than planned.

## Red flags

- Pressure to sign before you've seen the full costs in writing
- Requests for large fees in cash, or before any paperwork exists
- A broker or agent who can't show you an FSRA licence (you can look it up on FSRA's website)
- No conversation about how you'll get out of the mortgage
- Being asked to transfer title of your home to someone else
- Final terms that differ from what you were quoted
