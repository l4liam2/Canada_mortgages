A reverse mortgage is a loan against your home for Canadians aged 55 and older that doesn't require regular payments. For some retirees it's a sensible way to stay in a home they love while easing cash flow. For others it's an expensive answer to a problem with cheaper solutions.

## The basics

- **Who qualifies.** Homeowners 55 and older. If there are two owners on title, both generally need to meet the age requirement.
- **How much you can access.** It depends on your age (older borrowers can usually access more), where the home is, the type of property and its appraised value. The maximum is often up to about 55% of the home's value, and for many people it's less.
- **How you receive it.** A lump sum, scheduled advances, or a mix, depending on the lender.
- **Existing debt on the home.** Any current mortgage or HELOC generally has to be paid off first, usually from the reverse mortgage funds.

## No payments, but a growing balance

Because you aren't required to make regular payments, the interest is added to the balance, and then interest is charged on that interest. Over time the balance grows and your remaining equity shrinks.

As an illustration only, at 7% a year compounding roughly doubles a balance in about ten years. Reverse mortgage rates are higher than regular mortgage rates, so the longer the loan runs, the more this matters.

Many providers guarantee that you or your estate won't owe more than the home's fair market value when it's sold, as long as you've met the terms of the mortgage. Ask to see how that guarantee works.

## When it's repaid

The loan comes due when the home is sold, when you move out (including a move into long-term care), or when the last borrower passes away. Paying it off early can trigger a prepayment penalty, particularly in the first few years, so ask how that penalty is calculated before you sign.

## What you're still responsible for

You keep title to your home. You also keep the responsibilities of owning it: property taxes, home insurance and reasonable maintenance. Falling behind on these can put the reverse mortgage in default, so keep them in your budget.

## Who offers them

Only a small number of lenders offer reverse mortgages in Canada. The main providers are HomeEquity Bank, through its CHIP Reverse Mortgage, and Equitable Bank. Expect an appraisal and setup costs, and expect to get independent legal advice before signing, since it's a requirement. Because advances are loan proceeds rather than income, they generally aren't taxable, but confirm how they fit your own tax and benefits picture with an accountant.

## An honest comparison

**A HELOC.** Usually a lower rate, and you only pay interest on what you use. But you have to qualify on your income, interest has to be paid every month, and the lender can reduce or freeze the limit. If your retirement income comfortably covers the payments, a [HELOC](/services/heloc) is often the cheaper route.

**Downsizing.** Selling and moving somewhere smaller frees up equity with no interest at all. It has real costs (commission, land transfer tax on the next home, moving) and a real emotional cost, but over a long horizon it's often the least expensive option.

**Doing nothing.** If your income covers your needs, leaving the equity alone keeps it available for later, including care costs, and preserves it for your estate.

**A reverse mortgage.** It tends to fit best when you plan to stay in the home for many years, your income is tight, and monthly payments on a HELOC would be a strain.

## Bring family into the conversation

A reverse mortgage affects what's left in your estate, so it's worth including your adult children or whoever will handle your affairs. That isn't about asking permission.
